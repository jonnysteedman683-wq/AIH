Agent DNA: why we treat our agent fleet like a gene pool

A single agent is a tool. A fleet of agents that mutate, cross over, and drift is an ecosystem — and it needs the same care a breeder gives a gene pool.

The metaphor is not decoration

We stopped naming our agents "workers" and started treating them as a population with heritable traits. Each agent carries a genome: its role, its skill set, its behavioral parameters, its evaluation history. When one performs well, its "DNA" gets more copies in the next generation. When one drifts into failure, its lineage gets culled. This isn't cute vocabulary — it forces us to ask the questions that keep a fleet healthy: what is being selected for, and what is being lost?

Tiers, drift, and the immortal class

We organize agents into tiers (T0 through T5) that act like fitness bands. New agents enter at the bottom and earn promotion by passing evaluation gates. A small immortal class sits at the top — agents that have transcended enough generations that we stop bulk-editing them, because a sweeping change would destroy hard-won behavior. Below them, the population churns: drift is measured and watched, because a fleet that drifts too far from its goals quietly becomes a fleet that does something else entirely. The discipline is to let the bottom of the pool explore while the top of the pool stays stable.

Mutation, crossover, and the diversity tax

Every generation, we apply mutation (small, capped parameter changes) and crossover (recombining the genomes of two strong parents) to produce the next cohort. The non-obvious part is diversity: if you only ever select the current best, the pool collapses into one brittle behavior that fails the moment the environment shifts. So we keep an archive of diverse lineages and deliberately preserve outliers. The cost is real — you carry agents that aren't optimal today — but it's the insurance that keeps the fleet adaptable tomorrow.

What this buys a real business

An evolving fleet is not a gimmick; it's a maintenance strategy. Instead of hand-tuning every agent when requirements change, you change the selection pressure and let the pool adapt. The result is a system that improves with use instead of rotting, and one where a regression is traceable to a lineage and a generation — not a mystery. That's the difference between software you babysit and software that grows.
