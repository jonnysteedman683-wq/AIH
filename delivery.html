Clear scope.
No magic box.

Automation should make work easier to understand and safer to run—not hide important decisions behind an AI. Here’s the delivery approach I aim to agree with you before a project starts.

Discuss a workflow →

These are working standards and intended project practices, not a certification, guarantee or claim that every listed control is already running in a customer system. Project-specific scope is agreed before work begins.

The engagement

First, agree
what “done” means.

We start with the real workflow, not a preselected AI product.

01 / UNDERSTAND

Map the current process

We identify the people, systems, exceptions and handoffs involved. We look for the repetitive work and the cost of getting it wrong—not just the parts that are easy to automate.

02 / SCOPE

Write down boundaries

Before building, we agree the intended behavior, integrations, data involved, success checks, out-of-scope cases, expected handoff and price. If a rule or dependency is unclear, it stays a question—not an assumed requirement.

03 / BUILD

Keep consequential choices visible

We identify which steps can be automated and which need a person’s review or approval. Changes to scope, access or external effects are discussed rather than silently expanded.

04 / VERIFY

Check agreed paths and failure cases

We test the specific success paths and important edge cases listed in the scope. We record what was actually checked and call out anything blocked, skipped or still uncertain. A test pass is evidence for those checks—not proof of perfection.

05 / HAND OVER

Leave an understandable system

The handoff should describe what the automation does, how to operate it, where a human needs to step in, known limitations, and what to do when it fails. Any support or ongoing monitoring is a separate, explicitly scoped arrangement.

Boundaries are part of the design

Useful automation
knows its limits.

A

Human review for consequential actions

Where a wrong action could affect a customer, payment, legal obligation or important business decision, the need for a human checkpoint must be discussed and scoped. Do not assume an unattended action is acceptable.

B

Least necessary access

Integrations and data access should be limited to what the agreed workflow needs. Credentials, retention, permissions and vendor handling are project questions to resolve before connecting real systems.

C

Failure should be visible

We define what a failure looks like, who should know, and which work must stop for review. The system should report incomplete work honestly rather than quietly implying success.

D

Test without surprising real people

Tests that send messages, submit forms, charge money, alter live records or contact a production integration require explicit approval and a defined test plan. Prefer isolated or clearly marked test data when possible.

Honest expectations

What this does
not promise.

AI outputs can be wrong. Integrations can be unavailable or change. Tests cover the cases they actually exercise. No automation can promise zero errors, guaranteed revenue, uninterrupted service or compliance by itself.

Security, privacy, legal obligations, vendor terms and retention depend on the actual systems, data and jurisdiction involved. We should identify those constraints during scoping; specialist advice may be needed.

Ongoing monitoring, incident response, hosting, model/API spend and support are not implied by a one-time build. Any continuing responsibility must be agreed explicitly.
